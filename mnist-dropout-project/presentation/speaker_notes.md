# Speaker notes

1. **Question.** Ask the audience to predict which rate will yield the highest training accuracy, then which will recognize the most unseen digits. Keep the predictions visible until slide 9.
2. **Overfitting.** Follow the baseline training curve after its validation loss reaches a minimum. Explain how a model can become more confident in an incorrect answer even when the total number of errors barely moves.
3. **Paper.** Attribute the published 1.25% error to the authors’ three-layer ReLU model. Mention their wider networks and long training schedule. Introduce our smaller model as a way to isolate one design choice.
4. **Algorithm.** With drop probability 0.2, a hidden activation of 4 becomes 0 with probability 0.2 or 5 with probability 0.8. Its expectation stays 4. During evaluation it passes through as 4. Connect this arithmetic to the paper’s alternative scaling of outgoing weights at inference.
5. **Data.** Show a digit from each class and explain why the original 60,000 examples supply both training and validation data. The original 10,000 test digits enter only after checkpoint selection.
6. **Architecture.** Trace one pixel array through flattening and the two hidden layers. ReLU keeps positive responses. Explain why the classifier returns ten logits and why cross-entropy accepts them directly.
7. **Protocol.** Explain the paired seeds and independent shuffle generator. Holdout membership stays fixed. Collect 20 epochs even if validation peaks earlier. Select each checkpoint by minimum validation loss. Explain what the error bars measure.
8. **Code.** Show zero_grad, forward pass, cross-entropy, backward and optimizer.step in fit. Follow the call to score and point out eval and inference_mode. The accuracy gap compares the same network state on two sets.
9. **Results.** The baseline averaged 97.87% test accuracy. Dropping 20% of hidden activations raised it to 98.05%, and dropping 50% raised it to 98.09%. Both rates averaged about 98.03% validation accuracy. The 0.04 percentage-point test difference between them is small across three training seeds. Compare that with the seed SD. Explain the tradeoff between training fit and held-out performance. Use the three cohort rows to see whether the gain persists as sample size changes. Each smaller cohort also receives fewer optimizer steps.
10. **Evaluation.** In the seed-42 model with 0.2 dropout, 13 true nines were classified as fours. The next most frequent direction was five → three, with 11 cases. Compare that class’s recall with its precision. Point to an actual misclassification and describe the visible strokes. A plausible visual interpretation is a hypothesis about the error.
11. **Demo.** Run setup before the talk. In Draw a digit, identify the true label, read each prediction and compare the runner-up bars. Rerun the cell once. If all models agree, compare how much probability each places on alternatives. The fixed seed makes the sequence repeatable.
12. **Close.** Both dropout rates improved mean test accuracy and reduced the train–validation gap. The gap at selected checkpoints fell from 1.67 points without dropout to 1.40 points at a drop probability of 0.5. Limit the result to these rates, the fixed split and this architecture. Cite Srivastava et al. (2014), JMLR 15, 1929–1958 and the hojjatk MNIST archive on Kaggle, linked in the README.
